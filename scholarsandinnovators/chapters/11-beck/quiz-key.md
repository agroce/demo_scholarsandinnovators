## Part B — what a full-credit answer includes

**8.** Explore: many cheap experiments looking for an unexpected success; most fail. Expand: once something takes off, scale it as fast as possible, fixing the next bottleneck "just barely good enough." Extract: a mature product improved a percent at a time, forever. The value systems differ (risk tolerance, short vs. long term, how much there is to lose), so instincts that win in one phase lose in another. An explorer's speed creates fragility in extract; an extractor's caution kills exploration.

**9.** If people are punished for honest errors, they stop reporting errors and near-misses, so the organization loses the information it needs to fix the system that made the error possible. Firing the operator feels like action but leaves the causes in place (Dekker, Leveson). A blameless postmortem asks what in the tools, processes, and information allowed the failure, and produces fixes, while still holding people accountable for honesty: at Facebook, blaming others got you fired.

## Part C — rubric (10 points)

- **Explore (2):** Use AI freely for cheap experiments and prototypes. Vibe coding is fine; discard most of them. Don't mistake a demo for a product (Yang).
- **Expand (2):** Speed still matters, but add specifications (tests, property-based tests) as oracles. Review generated code for scaling and security bottlenecks (Hoodlet's ~45% vulnerability rate; skill attrition).
- **Extract (2):** Futures dominate. Keep the genie on "really tight reins," separate structural from behavioral changes (Tidy First), and require human design judgment.
- **Specifications as search (2):** Without an oracle, generation is unguided. The specification must evolve with what you learn.
- **Cross-chapter (2):** Yang's demos vs. maintenance; Hoodlet's experts vs. novices; Schneider's assurance and accountability.
