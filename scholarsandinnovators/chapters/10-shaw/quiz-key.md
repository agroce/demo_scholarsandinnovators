## Part B — what a full-credit answer includes

**8.** Every other discipline studies and critiques good examples before creating its own, which transmits tacit knowledge that rules don't capture. Software education skips this. Good exemplars are hard to find because systems are now big and complicated, reading the code doesn't convey the system's idea, documentation isn't kept up to date (her ADL-in-the-build idea was meant to fix that), and frameworks impose architectures you can't see or choose. She collects examples at modelproblems.org.

**9.** The required level of engineering and responsibility depends on both the consequence of failure and how much human oversight remains. Cruise control: the driver is attentive, so failure is bad but not a disaster. Adaptive cruise control: same consequence of a crash, less oversight. Self-driving: same consequence, even less oversight. Mission creep is a system's role expanding (cruise control "turned into hands-off driving") without its assurance rising to match.

## Part C — rubric (10 points)

- **Distinction (3):** Problem solving searches for a solution to a given, well-defined problem (Simon). Problem setting decides what the problem is and how to frame it (Schön); different disciplines frame it differently.
- **Wicked problems (2):** No definitive formulation; stakeholders with conflicting, sometimes unspoken agendas; no stopping rule; no second chance. Not merely "hard."
- **Application (3):** A concrete project analyzed with the concepts. For example, a benefits system: whose problem is it (claimants, caseworkers, legislators, auditors)? Accessibility and legal constraints can't be deferred (Kernighan/Pahlka); launching changes the situation itself.
- **What problem solving misses (2):** It optimizes a frame chosen too early, solves one stakeholder's version, and treats a requirements document as the problem (Kernighan's "waterfall as contract").
